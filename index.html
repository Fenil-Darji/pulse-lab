    Frequency spectrum

    How strongly each rhythm between 42 and 210 BPM is present in the pulse wave. The axis is in BPM and the tallest peak is the heart rate. A smaller bump at double that rate is the pulse's harmonic.

    How the page reads your pulse

    1. Average each frame. Every camera frame is shrunk to 40 × 40 pixels and its red, green and blue values are averaged. With light shining through your fingertip, red dominates.
    2. Check for a finger. Frames only count while they are strongly red. Lifting your finger restarts the 30 seconds.
    3. Drop the warm-up. The first 2 seconds after your finger lands are discarded while the camera adjusts its exposure.
    4. Even out the timing. Frames arrive at slightly uneven intervals, so the signal is resampled to exactly 30 samples per second.
    5. Keep heart-rate frequencies only. A band-pass filter keeps 0.7 to 3.5 Hz (42 to 210 BPM). It removes slow drift from breathing and hand movement and fast sensor noise.
    6. Find the rhythm two ways. The frequency spectrum gives the dominant rate. Separately, each beat's peak is located, and the gaps between beats give a second rate and the HRV figure.
    7. Grade the signal. Quality compares the power at the heart rate and its harmonic with everything else in the band, and checks that the two rates agree within 8%.

    Limits of this version

    • This is a learning tool, not a medical device.
    • The camera's automatic exposure keeps adjusting, which adds slow drift. The filter removes most of it.
    • Treat HRV as experimental. A 30 fps camera is coarse for beat-to-beat timing.
    • Movement and pressing too hard cause most bad readings.
    • Switching apps or locking the screen stops the measurement.
    Paste into a spreadsheet or Python to try your own filters.

    Measure breathing

    Phone on chest

    1. Turn the media volume up. The sonar plays a tone too high for most adults to hear.
    2. Lie on your back. Place the phone flat on your breastbone, screen up, with the bottom edge (speaker and microphone) pointing toward your chin.
    3. Rest your arms by your sides and breathe normally. A chime marks the start and the end of the 60-second window.
    4. To check accuracy, count each breath out between the two chimes, or ask someone to watch and count.
    1. Turn the media volume up.
    2. Sit back or lie down. Prop the phone 20 to 40 cm from your chest, bottom edge (speaker and microphone) pointing at your chest, for example on a pillow on your lap.
    3. Don't hold it. Hand movement is larger than breathing movement and swamps the sonar.
    4. Breathe normally. Count each breath out between the start and end chimes if you are checking accuracy.

    Children and pets may hear the sonar tone as a faint whine. Try a higher tone if it bothers anyone.

      How the page measures breathing

      1. Motion sensors. As your chest rises, the phone tilts by a fraction of a degree. The accelerometer sees gravity shift slightly between its axes, and the gyroscope sees the rotation. The page combines the axes into the one direction that moves the most.
      2. Sonar. The speaker plays a steady tone. The microphone hears it back, mixed with echoes. When your chest moves a few millimetres toward or away from the phone, the echo's timing shifts, which rotates the tone's phase. The page tracks that phase.
      3. Find the rhythm. Each signal is filtered to 6 to 42 breaths/min. Breaths are counted, and the frequency spectrum gives a second estimate.
      4. Grade each method. A method counts as clear when its signal repeats regularly from one breath to the next and its two estimates agree.
      5. Cross-check. If both methods are clear and within 2 breaths/min of each other, the result blends them, giving more weight to the cleaner signal. If they disagree, the cleaner one wins and the page says so.

      Limits of this version

      • This is a learning tool, not a medical device.
      • Talking, coughing and shifting position break the rhythm for a few breaths.
      • Phone speakers and microphones vary in how well they handle ultrasound. The live view shows how strongly the tone reaches the microphone.
      • Paying attention to your breathing tends to slow it. That is fine for testing, because the page and your count see the same breaths.

      Watch my posture

      Uses the front camera of this phone or laptop. A big sign in the top-right corner flashes while your posture needs fixing and turns green as soon as it is fixed. It keeps checking until you press End or close the tab.

      Setup

      1. Put the camera at about eye level, facing you. Prop a phone up rather than holding it.
      2. Sit 50 to 80 cm away, so your head and both shoulders are in view.
      3. When the check starts, sit up straight and look at the screen for 5 seconds. That posture becomes your target.
      4. Carry on working. Press End when you are done.

      The video is analysed on this device and never uploaded. The pose model (about 15 MB) downloads the first time.

      How the page checks posture

      1. Find your pose. A pose model (Google's MediaPipe Pose Landmarker) locates your nose, eyes, ears and shoulders in the camera image about 5 times a second.
      2. Learn your upright posture. During calibration it records where your head sits relative to your shoulders while you sit up straight.
      3. Compare every frame with that target. Six checks run at once:
        • Slouching: your head sinks toward your shoulders.
        • Head pushed forward: your head looks bigger compared with your shoulders, because it moved toward the camera.
        • Too close to the screen: your shoulders look wider than at calibration.
        • Looking down: your nose drops below the line of your ears.
        • Head tilted and shoulders uneven: the ear line or shoulder line tilts away from its calibrated angle.
      4. Warn after a delay. A problem has to last for the "Warn after" time before the sign turns red, so reaching for a cup does not set it off. Once you fix it, the sign turns green within 0.6 seconds, or within 2 seconds if you are only just inside the limit.

      Limits of this version

      • A single camera sees you from the front, so it judges posture from indirect clues. Treat it as a reminder, not a medical assessment.
      • Recalibrate after moving the camera or changing chairs, because the target depends on where the camera is.
      • Dim light, hair over the ears and a busy background make the pose less steady.
      • On a laptop it keeps checking in a background tab, but you only see the sign while the page is visible. Turn on the sound, or use the floating window in Chrome or Edge.
      • On a phone, the camera pauses when you switch apps, so keep the page on screen.

      Overnight snoring check

      The microphone listens while you sleep. In the morning the page shows how much of the night had snoring, and how often the snoring stopped for 10 seconds or more and then restarted with loud breaths. That stop-and-gasp pattern is the main sign of obstructive sleep apnoea.

      Only loudness is kept, 10 values a second. No sound is recorded, stored or uploaded.

      Before you sleep

      1. Plug the phone into its charger. A full night uses a lot of battery.
      2. Put it on the bed or bedside table, 30 to 50 cm from your head, bottom edge (microphone) towards you. Not under the pillow.
      3. Switch off fans and coolers if you can. Steady noise hides breathing sounds.
      4. Sleep alone in the bed if possible. A partner's snoring is counted too.
      5. Press Start and leave the page open. The screen stays on but turns almost black.
      6. In the morning, press End and see results.

      The night

      Loudness above the room's background through the night. Shaded stretches are steady snoring or loud breathing. Dots mark pauses of 10 seconds or more.

      STOP-Bang questionnaire

      Eight yes-or-no questions that doctors use to screen for obstructive sleep apnoea. It works with or without the recording, and the two together say more than either alone.

      How the page listens

      1. Loudness only. Ten times a second the microphone signal is reduced to one loudness number. The page never keeps the sound itself and cannot play back what it heard.
      2. Background level. For each minute the page finds the quiet end of the loudness, then smooths it over five minutes, so a fan or traffic changes the background rather than counting as snoring.
      3. Breath sounds. Sounds that rise 6 dB above the background and last 0.3 to 4 seconds count as a snore or loud breath. Longer sounds, such as talking, turning over or a door, count as other noise.
      4. Steady snoring. Three or more breath sounds in a row, 2 to 8 seconds apart.
      5. Pauses. Steady snoring, then 10 to 90 seconds with no breath sound and no other noise, then breathing sounds again.
      6. Rate. Pauses divided by the hours recorded. A clinic sleep study also counts shallow breathing that a microphone cannot hear, and divides by hours actually asleep, so this rate is not the same number as an AHI.

      Limits of this version

      • This is a learning tool, not a medical device. It has not yet been checked against a clinic sleep study.
      • It needs snoring to work. Someone with apnoea who breathes quietly will look like a quiet night.
      • A snoring partner, pets, fans, coolers and traffic all confuse it.
      • On some phones the microphone stops when the screen turns off or another app opens. The result says how much time was missed.
      • To check it, have someone sit up for 15 minutes and count the pauses they see, or record the same night as a sleep study.

      Cough counter

      The microphone listens for the sharp burst of a cough and counts them. Counting on several days shows whether a cough is getting better or worse, which helps a doctor more than "I cough a lot".

      Only loudness is kept, 50 values a second. No sound is recorded, stored or uploaded. The overnight Sleep recording counts coughs too.

      Setting up

      1. Put the phone within 2 metres of the person, microphone (bottom edge) towards them.
      2. A quiet room works best. Television, talking and other people coughing make the count less reliable.
      3. Leave the page open. For overnight counting, plug in the charger; the screen turns almost black.
      4. To check the count, tap the button on screen each time you hear a cough. The result compares your count with the page's.

      Child breathing count

      Fast breathing is the main sign health workers use to spot pneumonia in young children (WHO IMCI). Count while the child is calm or asleep, not crying or feeding: lift the clothes and watch the belly or chest, then tap once for every breath in.

      Danger signs: any one means go now

      How the page hears a cough

      1. Two loudness bands. Fifty times a second the page measures how loud the sound is below and above 1,000 Hz. Nothing else is kept.
      2. Background. The quiet end of the last 30 seconds sets the room's background level.
      3. A sharp start. A cough is a burst of air, so it goes from quiet to full loudness in about 40 milliseconds. Speech builds up more slowly, which is the main way the page tells them apart.
      4. Short and fading. It must last 0.14 to 0.75 seconds and fade after its peak. Claps and knocks are too short; snores, talking and music last too long.
      5. Rough sound. Coughs carry a lot of high-pitched noise compared with voiced speech or snoring.
      6. Quiet before. A cough follows a breath in, so the moment before it is quiet, unless it follows another cough in the same bout.

      Limits of this version

      • This is a learning tool, not a medical device. The cough rules were tuned on made-up sounds and have not yet been checked against real recordings.
      • Laughing, throat clearing, sneezing and some words can be counted as coughs. Coughs from other people in the room are counted too.
      • Use your own taps to check it, and compare sessions on the same phone in the same room.
      • The TB check and breathing count follow public health guidance, but only a health worker or doctor can diagnose TB or pneumonia.

      Tone hearing check

      Beeps play in one ear at a time, getting quieter, and you tap whenever you hear one. The page finds the quietest beep each ear hears at five pitches, from low (500 Hz) to high (6,000 Hz). It takes about 5 minutes.

      Before you start

      1. Find the quietest room you can. Switch off fans, coolers and the television.
      2. Use earphones or headphones, right side in the right ear. Wired ones are best; Bluetooth works if you always use the same pair.
      3. Turn the media volume to maximum and accept the volume warning. The beeps start quiet and are never louder than a normal conversation would be at that volume.
      4. Tap the big button every time you hear a beep, however faint. Don't guess when you hear nothing.

      Reference listener

      Phones and earphones play the same beep at different loudness, so the page cannot know on its own what counts as normal. Test a young family member (under 25) with good hearing once, with the same phone, earphones and maximum volume, and save their result as the reference. After that, everyone's result is compared with theirs and given a hearing grade.

      How the page tests hearing

      1. Ear check. One beep in each ear first, to make sure the earphones are the right way round and both sides work.
      2. Pulsed beeps. Each beep is three short pulses, which are easier to notice than one long tone and are not confused with ringing in the ears.
      3. Down 10, up 5. After every beep you hear, the next is 10 dB quieter; after every miss, 5 dB louder. The quietest level heard twice on the way up is that ear's threshold. Audiologists use the same steps.
      4. Random gaps and blank turns. Beeps come at irregular moments, and now and then nothing plays, to catch taps that are guesses.
      5. Repeat. The first pitch is tested again at the end of the right ear to see how steady the answers are.
      6. Grade. With a reference, the average over 500, 1,000, 2,000 and 4,000 Hz in the better ear is graded on the WHO scale: under 20 dB normal, 20 to 34 mild, 35 to 49 moderate, 50 to 64 moderately severe, 65 to 79 severe, 80 and above profound.

      Limits of this version

      • This is a learning tool, not a medical device, and not a substitute for a test by an audiologist.
      • The grade is only as good as the reference. A reference with an undiscovered hearing problem makes everyone look better than they are.
      • Background noise, loose earphones and a phone that lowers its volume on its own all raise the thresholds.
      • The left-right comparison does not need a reference, but cheap earphones can be a few dB louder on one side.
      • It does not test understanding speech in noise, which is often the first thing people notice.

      Distance vision

      A tumbling E chart, the kind used in eye camps, so it works for people who cannot read. The person stands back and points which way the E's arms face; a helper next to the phone taps the same arrow. Each eye is tested on its own.

      Setting up

      1. Turn the screen brightness to full. Use a well-lit room, without light shining on the screen.
      2. Prop the phone at the person's eye height, for example on a shelf or against books.
      3. Measure 3 metres (or 2) from the screen with a tape, and have the person stand there.
      4. The helper stands beside the phone and taps the arrow the person points to. Show them a big E first so they understand.

      Near vision, for reading glasses

      Hold the phone 40 cm from your eyes (a little less than elbow to fingertips), with both eyes open and your reading glasses on if you have them. Tap the arrow that matches the way the E's arms face. Difficulty with small print is common after 40 and easily fixed with reading glasses.

      Colour plates

      Six circles of coloured dots, each hiding a number. People with red-green colour blindness cannot see some of them. The first one everyone should see. These plates are generated by this page, not copies of the printed Ishihara book.

      Amsler grid

      A square grid with a dot in the middle, for spotting problems in the centre of the eye, such as macular degeneration or swelling from diabetes. Hold the phone about 30 cm away, cover one eye, keep looking at the dot, and tap any squares that look wavy, blurred, faded or missing.

      How the checks work

      1. Exact letter size. From the card match, the page knows how many screen pixels make a millimetre. A 6/6 E is drawn 5 minutes of arc tall at the chosen distance: 4.4 mm at 3 metres.
      2. Lines of five. Each size shows up to five Es in random directions. Three right passes the line; three wrong fails it.
      3. Finding the smallest line. The check starts at 6/24, steps two lines smaller while lines pass, then one at a time, until the line below the smallest passed line has failed.
      4. Referral. Worse than 6/12 in either eye (6/9 for school children) means an eye examination is needed, following WHO school and community screening guidance.
      5. Colour plates. The number differs from the background only in what the red-sensing or green-sensing cones see, so it disappears for someone missing that cone type. Dot brightness varies at random so brightness gives no clue.
      6. Amsler grid. Each square is 5 mm, which is 1 degree of vision at 30 cm.

      Limits of this version

      • This is a learning tool, not a medical device, and not a substitute for an eye examination.
      • Results depend on the card match, the distance and the room light. A wrong distance shifts the result by about a line per 25%.
      • Phone screens show colours differently, so the colour plates are rougher than printed plates.
      • It cannot detect glaucoma, cataract or diabetic eye disease directly. People with diabetes need a yearly eye examination whatever these checks show.
