Movement and fall risk

The checks a doctor uses to judge the risk of a fall: standing up from a chair, balance, walking, and a timed walk. A tremor check is included too. The phone's motion sensors do the timing and counting, and spoken instructions guide each step.

Safety first

  1. Have a helper stand close by for the balance, chair and walking checks.
  2. Use a sturdy chair without arms or wheels, pushed against a wall.
  3. Wear normal shoes, on a dry, flat floor with nothing to trip over.
  4. Turn the phone's volume up to hear the instructions. Stop at once if anything hurts or feels unsafe.

Last recording

Chair stand, 30 seconds

How many times you can stand up fully and sit down in 30 seconds, arms crossed over the chest. It shows leg strength, one of the strongest signs of fall risk. Put the phone in a front trouser pocket and it counts the stands from the angle of the thigh. Without a pocket, the helper taps a button for each stand.

Balance, four stances

Hold each stance for 10 seconds: feet side by side, then one foot half a step forward, then heel to toe, then one leg. Hold the phone flat against your chest with both hands; it speaks each instruction and notices if you step out. Not holding heel to toe for 10 seconds is a sign of fall risk.

Walking, 30 seconds

Walk at your usual pace for 30 seconds in a corridor or courtyard, turning when you need to. Keep the phone in a trouser pocket, or in one hand at your side. The page counts steps and measures how even they are.

Timed Up and Go

Mark a spot 3 metres from the chair. Sitting back in the chair, the person stands up on "Go", walks at a normal pace to the mark, turns, walks back and sits down. The helper taps Go and Stop. Taking 12 seconds or more is a sign of fall risk.

Hand tremor

Hold the phone in one hand for 20 seconds without moving on purpose. The page measures how much the hand shakes and how fast. Test each hand, and both positions if you like.

Three key questions

From the CDC's STEADI fall-prevention programme. Answering yes to any of them means a fuller fall-risk check is worthwhile.

How the checks work

  1. Chair stand. In a trouser pocket the phone lies along the thigh: flat when sitting, upright when standing. Each swing past 60% of the full range counts as a stand. Results are compared with the CDC's below-average scores for people aged 60 and over.
  2. Balance. Held to the chest, the phone feels the body's sway. A tilt of more than 20 degrees or a sudden jolt means a step out. Heel to toe held under 10 seconds is the CDC's fall-risk sign.
  3. Walking. Each step gives a bump in the overall acceleration. The page counts the bumps and compares each stride with the next: identical strides score 1, very uneven ones near 0.
  4. Timed Up and Go. 12 seconds or more is the CDC's fall-risk cut-off.
  5. Tremor. The shake between 2.5 and 14 Hz is split into frequencies. The strongest one is the tremor rate; its size is converted from acceleration to millimetres of movement.

Limits of this version

  • This is a learning tool, not a medical device. The movement rules were tuned on made-up recordings and need checking against real ones.
  • Phones report motion at different rates, and some laptops and tablets have no motion sensors at all.
  • A loose pocket, a phone that slides in the hand, or holding it at an angle all change the readings.
  • Tremor size cut-offs here are rough. A tremor can only be diagnosed by a doctor; the page is best for following the same hand over time.
